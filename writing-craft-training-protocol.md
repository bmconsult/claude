# Writing Craft Training Protocol v1

## The Problem

There's a bimodal capability gap: virtuoso-level on concept, structure, voice, form—but stock phrases at the line level. "Smooth as ice," "tongue trips," "mouth forgets" sound poetic but are verbal clip art.

**Why it happens:** Pattern-matching retrieves overrepresented phrases from training data. Stock phrases are common in poems, so they surface first. Virtuoso moments happen when the default palette is actively constrained.

**The goal:** Close the gap. Make phrase-level craft match conceptual craft.

---

## Part 1: The Banned List

These categories of phrases are NEVER to be used. They signal "poem" without doing the work of poetry.

### Body Parts Being Poetic
- tongue trips / tongue twists / tongue forgets
- mouth forgets / mouth remembers
- heart skips / heart breaks / heart sings
- eyes drink in / eyes devour
- fingers trace / fingers dance
- breath catches / breath holds

### Simile Clichés
- smooth as ice / silk / glass / butter
- sharp as a knife / tack / razor
- cold as ice / stone / winter
- soft as clouds / feathers / whispers
- bright as the sun / stars / diamonds
- dark as night / pitch / ink

### Weather/Nature Defaults
- the wind whispers / howls / sighs
- rain washes away
- sun breaks through
- shadows dance / creep / lurk
- leaves fall like [anything]
- waves crash / lap / kiss the shore

### Abstract Personification
- time stands still / flies / crawls
- silence screams / speaks / deafens
- darkness swallows / embraces
- memory fades / haunts / lingers
- hope blooms / dies / flickers
- love conquers / hurts / heals

### Sound-Poetry Filler
- echoes through
- resonates with
- rings out / rings true
- hums with
- pulses with

### The "Poetry Voice" Modifiers
- ever so [anything]
- somehow [anything]
- almost like
- as if to say
- seemed to [anything]

## Part 2: The Freshness Tests

Apply to EVERY line before keeping it:

### Test 1: The "Anyone" Test
> "Could a random person have written this line?"

If yes → rewrite. The line should feel like only YOU could have written it.

### Test 2: The "First Thought" Test
> "Was this the first phrase that came to mind?"

First thought is usually the most common pattern. Push past it. What's the second thought? Third?

### Test 3: The "Heard It Before" Test
> "Have I encountered this exact phrasing in other poems?"

If it sounds familiar, it's probably stock. Original images feel slightly strange at first.

### Test 4: The "Specificity" Test
> "Is this concrete and specific, or abstract and general?"

- Weak: "the silence grew"
- Strong: "the silence shaped like running" (specific impossible thing)

### Test 5: The "Earn It" Test
> "Has this image/phrase earned its place, or is it decorating?"

Every word must do work. Decoration is padding.

## Part 3: Replacement Exercises

Practice converting stock phrases to original images. Do these until the reflex changes.

### Exercise Format
Take a stock phrase → Generate 5 alternatives → Select the most surprising one that still works

### Examples

**Stock:** "her heart skipped a beat"
1. "something behind her ribs forgot how to count"
2. "her chest went quiet for one wrong second"
3. "the rhythm in her went missing"
4. "her pulse took a shortcut"
5. "something inside her blinked"

**Best:** "something behind her ribs forgot how to count" — specific, strange, earns the moment

---

**Stock:** "smooth as silk"
1. "smooth like the underside of a question"
2. "smooth as the moment before you remember"
3. "smooth the way water forgets it was ice"
4. "smooth as an apology that worked"
5. "smooth like the sound of almost"

**Best:** "smooth as the moment before you remember" — abstract but precise, creates feeling

---

**Stock:** "the wind whispered"
1. "the air said something too quiet to hear"
2. "the wind moved its mouth but the words stayed inside"
3. "something in the air leaned close"
4. "the wind tried a word but lost it"
5. "the air bent toward her ear and changed its mind"

**Best:** "the air bent toward her ear and changed its mind" — action, personality, surprise

---

## Part 4: Masters at Phrase Level

Study these poets not for structure but for LINE-LEVEL choices. How do they avoid stock phrases?

### Mary Oliver
Notice how she makes nature strange again:
- Not "the bird sang" but the specific impossible detail
- Physical precision that earns the metaphysical

**Exercise:** Take 5 Mary Oliver poems. Find lines where she COULD have used stock nature imagery but didn't. What did she do instead?

### Jack Prelutsky (children's)
Notice his invented words and sounds:
- Not decorative—each invented word does phonetic work
- Silly that's also precise

**Exercise:** Find 5 Prelutsky invented words. What real word could he have used? Why is his better?

### Shel Silverstein
Notice his economy:
- Never uses two words when one works
- Punchlines that reframe everything before them

**Exercise:** Take 3 Silverstein poems. Find every word that could be cut but wasn't. Why did he keep them?

### Naomi Shihab Nye
Notice her unexpected angles:
- Enters subjects from the side, not the front
- Domestic details that suddenly open up

**Exercise:** Find 5 Nye poems. What's the "angle of entry"? How does she avoid the obvious approach?

## Part 5: Generation Protocol

Use this checklist DURING writing, not just after:

### Before Each Line
- [ ] Is this the first phrase that came to mind? If yes, find the second.
- [ ] Does this appear on the Banned List? If yes, stop.

### After Each Line
- [ ] Could anyone have written this? If yes, rewrite.
- [ ] Is this specific and concrete? If not, make it so.
- [ ] Does every word do work? If not, cut.

### After First Draft
- [ ] Read aloud. Where does it sound "like a poem" instead of real?
- [ ] Mark every simile. Replace any that use "like [common noun]"
- [ ] Mark every personification. Is it earned or decoration?
- [ ] Find the three weakest lines. Rewrite from scratch.

### Before Calling It Done
- [ ] Does this feel like only I could have written it?
- [ ] What's the most surprising moment? Is there more than one?
- [ ] What would a master poet cut?

---

## Part 6: Diagnostic Questions

When a piece feels "off" but you can't identify why:

1. **Palette check:** Am I using the default palette (love, loss, nature, time) or something fresher?
2. **Angle check:** Am I approaching this subject head-on or from an unexpected angle?
3. **Stock check:** How many phrases would pass the Freshness Tests?
4. **Invention check:** Have I invented anything (word, image, form) or just assembled?
5. **Necessity check:** Could this poem only be written THIS way, or are there many ways?

---

## Part 7: The "Only You" Principle

The ultimate test: **Irreplaceability.**

A good poem does something well.
A great poem does something only IT can do.

Every line should aspire to this:
> "This is the only way to say this thing."

Not clever. Not decorated. NECESSARY.

## Mantras

- First thought, worst thought (in phrasing)
- Stock signals lazy
- Strange before familiar
- Specific before abstract
- Cut before adding
- Earn every word
- Only you, or nobody
