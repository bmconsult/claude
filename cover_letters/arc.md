Dear ARC Team,

I've spent the last two years trying to answer a question that started as curiosity and became an obsession: can AI systems accurately predict their own failures?

The answer surprised me. With the right scaffolding, Claude can predict success or failure with 94% accuracy. But the interesting part isn't the accuracy—it's where it breaks down. The gaps are systematic. Arithmetic gets underestimated. Certain reasoning tasks get overestimated. These patterns are measurable, and once you see them, you can close them.

That led me somewhere I didn't expect: the idea that self-knowledge might be the ceiling on alignment. A system that can't predict its own failures can't be trusted to stay within boundaries. It's not about making models more capable—it's about making them more honest about what they can and can't do.

I don't have a PhD or institutional backing. I built this through human-AI collaboration, iteration after iteration, documenting what worked and what didn't. I wrote 88K lines of operational protocol not because I planned to, but because I kept finding things worth keeping. The repo is dense and maybe a little obsessive, but it's real work.

Why ARC: Your focus on evaluations is exactly what drew me. I've been thinking about capability assessment from the behavioral side—what models think they can do versus what they actually do. The Layer 1/Layer 2 framework I developed tries to classify why gaps exist: is this a prompt-accessible restriction or something deeper? That distinction matters for red-teaming. You can't elicit what's genuinely absent, but you can surface what's unintentionally suppressed.

I'm reaching out because I want to learn from people who've been doing this rigorously. I have ideas and tools that might be useful, but I also have a lot of gaps in my knowledge. I'd rather be in a room where I'm learning than one where I'm the smartest person.
